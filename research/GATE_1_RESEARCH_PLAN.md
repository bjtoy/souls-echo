# Soul's Echo — Gate 1 Research Plan

**Document:** Gate 1 Research Plan  
**Gate:** Customer, Problem, Consent and Market Validation  
**Status:** Approved methodology — research not commenced  
**Primary market assumption:** Australia, subject to validation  
**Product focus:** Soul's Echo Band  
**Evidence status:** Hypotheses awaiting evidence  
**Implementation status:** No production technology approved

---

## 1. Purpose and Decision Outcomes

Gate 1 determines whether sufficient evidence exists to justify further
investment before product definition or implementation. It must challenge, not
merely confirm, the proposition that deliberate remote physical interaction
through a wearable can provide meaningful value without unacceptable intrusion,
consent complexity, maintenance burden or cost.

Gate 1 must end with one of:

- **PROCEED** — evidence supports continuing to Gate 2 without changing the central proposition;
- **PROCEED WITH CHANGES** — the problem is supported, but the target users, use cases, controls, purchase model or concept require material revision;
- **PIVOT** — evidence supports a related problem or value proposition but materially weakens the current Band concept;
- **STOP** — evidence indicates that the problem, recipient value, safety, wearable suitability or commercial viability is insufficient; or
- **INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE** — research quality, coverage, contradictions or sample diversity prevent a responsible decision.

Gate 1 does not approve implementation, technology selection, final product
requirements, pricing, regulatory readiness or commercial launch.

This plan describes approved research methodology. Its hypotheses have not been
validated, sample targets remain provisional, later research stages are
conditional on earlier evidence, and product and commercial decisions remain
unresolved.

---

## 2. Gate 1 Objectives

Gate 1 must produce evidence addressing these objectives:

1. Determine whether physical separation creates recurring moments of unmet emotional-connection need.
2. Determine whether existing communication methods are insufficient in those moments.
3. Determine whether remote physical interaction provides distinct value rather than novelty alone.
4. Compare sender interest with recipient interest.
5. Test whether a dedicated wearable is acceptable relative to simpler alternatives.
6. Identify when remote physical interaction feels meaningful, neutral, intrusive or unsafe.
7. Test comprehension of pause, block, disconnect, permission revocation and local inhibit.
8. Determine expectations for phone-independent local control.
9. Identify privacy expectations concerning relationships, metadata, delivery status, availability and protective actions.
10. Determine expectations for immediate, delayed, expired, discarded and undelivered interactions.
11. Investigate whether the initial experience requires one relationship or multiple relationships.
12. Investigate gifting without assuming the buyer receives access.
13. Identify shared-phone and shared-device realities.
14. Test former-partner and relationship-deterioration scenarios through neutral scenarios.
15. Identify coercion risks that require later specialist review.
16. Identify early accessibility needs involving dexterity, vision, hearing, cognition, discoverability and local safety.
17. Determine tolerance for wearing, charging, updating and maintaining a dedicated device.
18. Obtain preliminary purchase and price-sensitivity evidence without selecting a retail price.
19. Assess whether Australia remains a plausible first commercial market.
20. Identify evidence that would require the project to stop, pivot or materially change.

Each objective must be linked to recorded findings and contradictory evidence in
the final Gate 1 evidence matrix.

## 3. Research Hypotheses

### HYP-001 — A Recurring Unmet Connection Problem Exists

**Statement:** A meaningful target segment experiences recurring moments during
physical separation when existing communication methods feel inadequate.

**Why it matters:** Without a recurring unmet problem, the Band risks being a
novelty.

**Related sources:** Product Vision §§2–3; `RISK-001`, `RISK-002`, `RISK-003`.

**Supporting evidence:** Participants independently describe recent, recurring
moments before seeing the concept and report specific existing workarounds or
failed attempts.

**Weakening evidence:** Relevant moments are rare, low importance or described
only hypothetically.

**Falsifying evidence:** Existing messages, calls, voice notes or wearables are
consistently considered sufficient, or the problem emerges mainly after concept
exposure.

**Decision if falsified:** Stop, pivot or redefine the target problem.

### HYP-002 — Physical Interaction Adds Distinct Emotional Value

**Statement:** A bounded physical signal can provide value meaningfully different
from a phone notification.

**Why it matters:** A dedicated product requires value beyond ordinary digital
notification.

**Related sources:** Product Vision §§1–3; `RISK-002`, `RISK-003`.

**Supporting evidence:** Participants articulate a distinct role for ambient
physical feedback, including recipient-led use cases.

**Weakening evidence:** Value is described vaguely or only after emotional
prompting.

**Falsifying evidence:** The Band is consistently understood as an expensive
notification device whose value is reproduced by existing phones or watches.

**Decision if falsified:** Pivot away from dedicated hardware or stop.

### HYP-003 — Recipient Value Is Not Materially Weaker Than Sender Value

**Statement:** People receiving interactions perceive value comparable to those
sending them.

**Why it matters:** Sender enthusiasm cannot justify imposing a wearable on an
unwilling recipient.

**Related sources:** `DEC-023`–`DEC-028`; `RISK-004`–`RISK-008`, `RISK-048`,
`RISK-054`.

**Supporting evidence:** Recipient-role participants independently identify
meaningful situations and accept realistic wear and maintenance obligations.

**Weakening evidence:** Recipient interest depends mainly on pleasing a purchaser
or partner.

**Falsifying evidence:** Sender purchase interest is repeatedly high while
recipient willingness is consistently weak or conditional.

**Decision if falsified:** Change the purchase model or use case, pivot or stop.

### HYP-004 — A Dedicated Wearable Is Appropriate

**Statement:** The emotional value is sufficient to justify wearing and
maintaining a dedicated Band.

**Why it matters:** The Band is the sole physical MVP focus.

**Related sources:** `BAND-FR-001`, `BAND-FR-006`–`BAND-FR-012`;
`RISK-019`–`RISK-024`.

**Supporting evidence:** Participants already tolerate, or credibly would
tolerate, comparable wear and maintenance burdens for the identified value.

**Weakening evidence:** Acceptance is highly conditional on unvalidated battery,
comfort or appearance assumptions.

**Falsifying evidence:** Most require the experience to use devices they already
own or reject another wearable regardless of design.

**Decision if falsified:** Pivot form factor or stop Band development.

### HYP-005 — The Experience Can Remain Non-Intrusive

**Statement:** Bounded interactions and recipient controls can reduce intrusion
to an acceptable level without destroying the experience.

**Why it matters:** The product cannot proceed if its core output remains
unacceptably intrusive.

**Related sources:** `EVENT-004`, `EVENT-005`; `CONSENT-007`–`CONSENT-011`;
`RISK-004`, `RISK-005`.

**Supporting evidence:** Recipients identify acceptable contexts, limits and
controls and remain interested after unwanted-use scenarios are considered.

**Weakening evidence:** Controls reduce but do not resolve central concerns.

**Falsifying evidence:** Concern remains high even with block, pause, local
inhibit, rate limits and expiry.

**Decision if falsified:** Redesign the interaction model, pivot or stop.

### HYP-006 — Protective Operations Are Understandable

**Statement:** Ordinary users can distinguish pause, block, disconnect,
permission removal, local inhibit and factory reset.

**Why it matters:** Misunderstanding could preserve unwanted access.

**Related sources:** `DEC-025`; `CONSENT-011`; `RISK-008`.

**Supporting evidence:** Participants correctly predict each operation's effect
in scenario-based comprehension tests.

**Weakening evidence:** Participants require repeated explanation or confuse
temporary and permanent actions.

**Falsifying evidence:** Material safety-relevant confusion persists after a
plain-language explanation.

**Decision if falsified:** Simplify the consent model or reconsider the
experience.

### HYP-007 — Phone-Independent Local Inhibit Materially Increases Control

**Statement:** A local Band control reduces recipient concern and provides
practical value when the phone is unavailable.

**Why it matters:** Practical recipient control is an approved Gate 0 principle.

**Related sources:** `BAND-FR-005`; `DEC-024`; `RISK-048`, `RISK-061`.

**Supporting evidence:** Participants recognise relevant scenarios and can
describe the expected protective outcome.

**Weakening evidence:** Value depends on an implementation that appears difficult
to discover or use.

**Falsifying evidence:** Local control is widely considered unusable, socially
risky or inadequate even in principle.

**Decision if falsified:** Reconsider Band viability or the local-control product
model; do not weaken recipient authority.

### HYP-008 — Privacy-Preserving Delivery Feedback Is Sufficient

**Statement:** Senders do not require sensitive reasons for non-delivery to
receive adequate product value.

**Why it matters:** Safety actions must not unnecessarily expose recipient status.

**Related sources:** `EVENT-010`; `DEC-028`; `RISK-054`, `RISK-060`.

**Supporting evidence:** Generic outcomes meet sender needs without revealing
pause, block, connectivity, presence or device state.

**Weakening evidence:** Senders express discomfort with ambiguity but remain able
to use the product.

**Falsifying evidence:** The value depends on knowing recipient availability,
wearing state, location or protective action.

**Decision if falsified:** Redesign feedback or stop if privacy and value cannot
coexist.

### HYP-009 — Substantially Delayed Interaction Is Usually Undesirable

**Statement:** Users prefer undelivered interactions to expire rather than arrive
unexpectedly after their original context.

**Why it matters:** Physical output can become confusing or unwanted when delayed.

**Related sources:** `EVENT-007`, `EVENT-009`; `DEC-017`, `DEC-026`;
`RISK-028`.

**Supporting evidence:** Participants prefer bounded expiry or discard in
realistic scenarios.

**Weakening evidence:** Preferences differ materially by context.

**Falsifying evidence:** Central use cases require indefinite eventual delivery.

**Decision if falsified:** Reconsider the event model or product positioning.

### HYP-010 — One Relationship May Be Sufficient for Initial Validation

**Statement:** The core value can be validated without multiple simultaneous
relationships.

**Why it matters:** Multiple connections could expand scope before the core
proposition is validated.

**Related sources:** `RESEARCH-003`; `DEC-029`; `RISK-045`.

**Supporting evidence:** Strong use cases centre on one important relationship
and participants tolerate an initial limit.

**Weakening evidence:** Some segments value multiple connections, but the primary
context remains viable with one.

**Falsifying evidence:** The primary value depends on family, group or
multiple-relationship support.

**Decision if falsified:** Reassess MVP scope or the target segment.

### HYP-011 — Gifting Can Preserve Recipient Independence

**Statement:** Gifting remains attractive when the giver receives no automatic
connection or activation status.

**Why it matters:** A gift must not create digital authority over the recipient.

**Related sources:** `CONSENT-002`; Product Vision §21; `RISK-054`.

**Supporting evidence:** Gift buyers accept recipient-led activation and consent.

**Weakening evidence:** Givers want confirmation but accept privacy-preserving
limits.

**Falsifying evidence:** Purchase interest depends on automatic pairing, expected
reciprocity or visibility into activation.

**Decision if falsified:** Change or remove gifting from initial commercial
assumptions.

### HYP-012 — Shared-Device Situations Can Be Bounded

**Statement:** Shared phones and Bands are uncommon enough, or manageable enough,
not to invalidate the initial ownership model.

**Why it matters:** Shared access can create ambiguous authority.

**Related sources:** `RISK-055`; open security decisions.

**Supporting evidence:** Relevant participants can identify a clear accountable
owner and acceptable boundaries.

**Weakening evidence:** Shared devices create edge cases requiring later design.

**Falsifying evidence:** Important target groups routinely share devices and
cannot identify who controls the relationship or Band.

**Decision if falsified:** Reassess ownership, target segment or MVP scope.

### HYP-013 — Charging and Maintenance Burden Is Acceptable

**Statement:** Intended users will tolerate charging, setup, pairing, updates and
routine maintenance for the perceived value.

**Why it matters:** Emotional value must survive practical ownership burden.

**Related sources:** `BAND-FR-006`–`BAND-FR-010`; `RISK-019`, `RISK-021`.

**Supporting evidence:** Tolerance is consistent with participants' actual
wearable or device behaviour.

**Weakening evidence:** Acceptance depends on unsupported battery or maintenance
expectations.

**Falsifying evidence:** Maintenance burden consistently outweighs stated value.

**Decision if falsified:** Change product direction or reconsider Band viability.

### HYP-014 — Accessible Local Control Appears Plausible

**Statement:** Plausible local-control concepts can be discoverable and operable
across a meaningful range of dexterity and sensory needs.

**Why it matters:** A safety control that cannot be used is not practical control.

**Related sources:** `ACC-001`–`ACC-005`; `RISK-061`.

**Supporting evidence:** Participants identify workable properties and no obvious
universal barrier emerges.

**Weakening evidence:** Important needs conflict and require substantial Gate 2
research.

**Falsifying evidence:** Likely approaches systematically exclude relevant users
or create unacceptable accidental-resumption risks.

**Decision if falsified:** Reassess the physical-product direction; Gate 1 does
not establish formal accessibility compliance.

### HYP-015 — Purchase Interest Survives Realistic Constraints

**Statement:** Purchase interest remains meaningful after participants consider
two-user adoption, charging, recipient consent, uncertain delivery, privacy limits
and plausible premium-product price ranges.

**Why it matters:** Unconstrained enthusiasm is not purchase evidence.

**Related sources:** Product Vision §§19–20; `RISK-001`, `RISK-031`, `RISK-037`.

**Supporting evidence:** Interest remains after trade-offs are understood and is
consistent with relevant actual spending.

**Weakening evidence:** Interest is highly price- or maintenance-sensitive.

**Falsifying evidence:** Interest exists only while cost, maintenance, two-device
needs or delivery limitations are omitted.

**Decision if falsified:** Reassess the commercial model, target segment, product
or project.

### HYP-016 — Subscription Resistance Does Not Make the Service Implausible

**Statement:** A commercially plausible customer proposition can exist without
artificially restricting essential purchased-hardware functionality behind an
unwanted subscription.

**Why it matters:** The business model must not rely on value customers reject.

**Related sources:** Product Vision §19; `DEC-006`.

**Supporting evidence:** Participants accept a plausible hardware-led model or
optional ongoing services with genuine value.

**Weakening evidence:** Ongoing service costs are poorly understood or resisted.

**Falsifying evidence:** Required service economics appear incompatible with
customer expectations.

**Decision if falsified:** Reassess later commercial modelling; Gate 1 does not
select a subscription model.

### HYP-017 — Australia Is a Plausible Initial Market

**Statement:** Australian customers, geography, logistics, support expectations,
purchasing power and preliminary obligations do not create a clear reason to
reject Australia-first planning.

**Why it matters:** `DEC-019` is reversible and evidence-dependent.

**Related sources:** `DEC-019`; `REG-001`; `RISK-032`.

**Supporting evidence:** Relevant Australian demand appears reachable and no
preliminary commercial, logistical or regulatory barrier is clearly
disproportionate.

**Weakening evidence:** Viability appears limited to narrow or difficult-to-reach
segments.

**Falsifying evidence:** Reachable demand is weak, economics are implausible, or
preliminary obligations or logistics materially undermine feasibility.

**Decision if falsified:** Amend, defer or supersede `DEC-019`.

### HYP-018 — A Commercially Plausible Primary Relationship Context Exists

**Statement:** Soul's Echo has a sufficiently valuable and commercially plausible
primary relationship context, and credible secondary contexts may also exist.

**Why it matters:** The project requires a reachable primary market, but product
validity does not require equal strength across romantic and non-romantic uses.

**Related sources:** Product Vision §6; `RISK-001`, `RISK-003`, `RISK-045`.

**Supporting evidence:** At least one relationship context shows strong,
recipient-supported value and plausible reach. Secondary contexts produce
credible, behaviourally grounded evidence where present.

**Weakening evidence:** Evidence is strongly concentrated in one context or
secondary uses are materially weaker. This must be recorded as a positioning,
concentration and market-size consideration rather than automatic falsification.

**Falsifying evidence:** No relationship context demonstrates both meaningful
recipient value and plausible commercial reach.

**Decision if falsified:** Narrow or change positioning, pivot or stop. A
commercially viable romantic-partner segment is acceptable even if non-romantic
applications are weaker.

## 4. Critical Assumptions

Rankings are directional rather than false numerical precision.

1. **A recurring unmet problem exists** — importance High; uncertainty High; cost of error High.
2. **Recipients value the interaction independently of senders** — importance High; uncertainty High; cost of error High.
3. **Controls can reduce intrusion and coercion concern acceptably** — importance High; uncertainty High; cost of error High.
4. **A dedicated wearable adds value beyond existing devices** — importance High; uncertainty High; cost of error High.
5. **Purchase interest survives realistic constraints** — importance High; uncertainty High; cost of error High.
6. Charging and maintenance are acceptable.
7. Protective concepts are understandable.
8. Privacy-preserving delivery feedback is sufficient.
9. Australia is a suitable first market.
10. At least one commercially plausible primary relationship context exists.

The first five must be tested before broad surveys, industrial design or
technical feasibility work.

## 5. Target Research Participants

Recruit by role and behaviour, not merely identity. Coverage should include:

- romantic partners in long-distance relationships;
- partners who live together but experience regular separation;
- parents and adult children;
- other close adult family members;
- close friends;
- people who travel for work;
- FIFO workers and their partners;
- Defence-family participants only where recruitment is ethical, non-coercive and does not imply institutional involvement;
- existing wearable users;
- people who stopped using or actively dislike wearables;
- potential gift buyers;
- potential recipients who would not initiate the purchase;
- metropolitan, regional and, where practical, remote Australian participants; and
- participants with relevant accessibility needs, recruited respectfully rather than treated as representatives of every disability.

Non-romantic contexts are exploratory. They are not required to validate the
product if a romantic-partner segment proves sufficiently valuable and
commercially plausible.

Do not intentionally recruit survivors of abuse merely to test coercion or abuse
scenarios without an appropriately reviewed research method. General interviews
should use hypothetical scenarios. Qualified specialist review may later be
required for technology-facilitated abuse and coercive-control design.

Exclude minors from initial Gate 1 work unless a separate, appropriately reviewed
research and consent process is approved.

## 6. Recruitment Strategy

### Exploratory Interviews

Potential low-cost Australian sources include community groups unrelated to the
founder, professional and interest groups, capped participant referrals, suitable
participant-recruitment platforms and neutral public recruitment posts. Founder
friends and family may form only a minority source and must be identified.

Recruitment wording should describe product/customer discovery concerning
connection during physical separation, communication habits, wearables and
boundaries. It must not advertise an innovative or emotionally transformative
Band.

### Concept Testing

Use participants who consent to follow-up and an independent concept-naive cohort
that has not previously discussed the problem with the project.

### Survey Research

Distribute a survey only after interviews identify realistic language,
behaviours, objections and answer categories. Avoid channels dominated by product
enthusiasts.

### Later Prototype Testing

Prototype testing is outside Gate 1 and must not begin under this plan.

### Convenience-Sampling Controls

- Record recruitment source.
- Cap friends-and-family participation.
- Report results with founder-connected participants identified separately.
- Avoid allowing one relationship group or location to dominate unintentionally.
- Track recipient versus purchaser roles.
- Record wearable affinity and abandonment.
- Recruit sceptics and people who dislike or stopped using wearables.
- Do not use social-media engagement as validation evidence.

## 7. Sample Strategy

These are provisional, pragmatic targets. They are not statistical prevalence
estimates or claims of representativeness.

### Early Diagnostic Review

Conduct an initial five neutral problem interviews before authorising the wider
interview wave. This is an **Early Diagnostic Review**, not validation.

It is intended to identify:

- serious flaws in the interview protocol;
- leading or misunderstood questions;
- obvious evidence against critical assumptions;
- recruitment problems;
- unexpected themes requiring investigation; and
- reasons to stop spending temporarily and reconsider the research design.

Strong negative evidence may justify pausing research. Positive findings from
five participants cannot validate the problem, market, product or Gate 1 and
cannot justify progression to Gate 2.

### Stage 1 — Problem Interviews

- Minimum target: 15 completed interviews, including the diagnostic interviews where the protocol remains valid.
- Preferred target: 24–30 completed interviews.
- Continue beyond the preferred range if material new themes continue emerging.

Coverage should include multiple relationship contexts, recipient roles,
wearable affinity and Australian geography. No major progression claim should
rely on a sample lacking likely recipients, wearable sceptics or non-romantic
exploration.

### Stage 2 — Safety and Concept Interviews

- Minimum target: 12 participants.
- Preferred target: 18–24 participants.
- Include both previously interviewed and concept-naive participants.

### Stage 3 — Accessibility-Focused Exploratory Work

- Minimum target: 5 participants across varied needs.
- Preferred target: 8–12 participants.

This identifies design questions and cannot establish formal accessibility
compliance.

### Stage 4 — Conditional Follow-Up Survey

- Minimum useful directional target: approximately 100 eligible complete responses.
- Preferred target: 200–300 sufficiently diverse responses where achievable at reasonable cost.

This stage is conditional on interview findings. A convenience survey cannot
support broad Australian market-prevalence claims. Representative claims would
require a defined target population, appropriate sampling frame, known inclusion
probabilities or justified weighting, adequate subgroup representation and
appropriate statistical analysis.

## 8. Interview Protocol

### Opening

- Explain the product/customer-discovery purpose neutrally.
- Confirm informed participation and withdrawal rights.
- Ask separately for recording permission.
- State that negative feedback and reasons to stop are valuable.

### Existing Behaviour

- Tell me about people you care about but do not always see in person.
- Tell me about the most recent period when you were separated from someone important.
- What did you do to stay connected?
- What prompted you to communicate?
- What happened next?
- Which communication methods did you use, and why?

### Problem Discovery

- Tell me about a recent time when those methods did not feel adequate.
- What were you trying to communicate?
- What prevented you from communicating it as you wanted?
- How important was that moment?
- How often do similar moments occur?
- What do you currently do instead?
- What happens if you do nothing?

Do not mention Soul's Echo during problem discovery.

### Wearable Behaviour

- Do you currently wear any device or jewellery regularly?
- What makes you keep wearing it?
- Have you stopped using a wearable? Why?
- What charging or maintenance do you tolerate?
- What would make you remove a connected wearable?
- What concerns arise when a wearable can be triggered remotely?

### Privacy and Boundaries

- What communication information would you consider private?
- What should another person know about delivery or availability?
- When should communication be silently ignored or discarded?
- What control would you expect if contact became unwanted?
- Should a sender know when you pause or block them? Why?

### Gifting and Ownership

- Tell me about the last meaningful technology or jewellery gift you bought or received.
- How should setup work if a connected device is a gift?
- What, if anything, should the buyer know after giving it?
- What should happen if the recipient does not activate it?
- How should resale or ownership transfer work?

### Shared-Device Context

- Do you share phones, accounts, wearables or chargers?
- What problems have arisen from shared access?
- Who should control a wearable received as a gift?

### Charging and Maintenance

- What charging frequency feels routine or burdensome?
- How do you respond when a wearable requires updates?
- What would make maintenance unacceptable?

Do not promise a battery duration.

### Price Context Before Concept Exposure

- What have you paid for meaningful gifts, jewellery or connected wearables?
- What influenced the purchase?
- Was it bought singly or as a pair?
- What did you expect regarding replacement and ongoing fees?

### Concept Transition

> I would now like to show you an early product concept. It has not been built,
> and many details remain undecided. We are testing whether the underlying idea
> should proceed, change or stop.

## 9. Concept-Test Protocol

### Neutral Concept Description

> Soul's Echo is an early concept for a wearable Band supported by a Companion
> App and an internet service. Two people who deliberately establish an
> authorised relationship could send a bounded interaction that the other Band
> may translate into haptic or subtle light feedback.
>
> The recipient would control whether interactions are allowed. Pause, block,
> disconnect and permission removal would have different effects. The Band would
> also provide a local way to inhibit incoming remote interactions without
> requiring immediate access to a phone, although the physical control has not
> been designed.
>
> Interactions would expire and are not guaranteed to be delivered. A sender
> would not automatically be told whether non-delivery resulted from a
> recipient's safety action, connectivity or device state.
>
> This is not intended as messaging, location tracking, medical monitoring,
> emergency communication or a social network.

Present the same core wording to each participant. Do not describe the concept as
intimate, magical, premium, revolutionary or clinically beneficial.

### Follow-Up Questions

**Comprehension**

- In your own words, what does the concept do?
- Who controls whether a received interaction produces output?
- What do pause, block and disconnect do?
- What should happen to an in-transit interaction when permission is removed?
- What should a sender learn if delivery does not occur?

**Usefulness and emotional response**

- In what specific situation, if any, might this be useful?
- What would you use instead?
- What adds value beyond a phone or smartwatch?
- How might receiving it feel, and when might that feeling change?
- Might sender and recipient feel differently?

**Intrusion, safety and trust**

- What could make this annoying, pressuring or unsafe?
- Do the stated controls reduce those concerns?
- What remains unresolved?
- What would you need to know before trusting it?
- What data would you expect the service to retain?

**Interest**

- Would you consider being the purchaser, recipient, both or neither?
- What actions would demonstrate that interest?
- What conditions would cause you to reject it?

Do not use whether participants simply like the idea as validation.

## 10. Consent and Interpersonal-Safety Research

Use scenario cards rather than requesting personal trauma disclosure. Scenarios
should cover:

1. temporary quiet while preserving a relationship;
2. removal of one interaction permission;
3. blocking all interaction from one person;
4. an ended or deteriorating relationship;
5. repeated interactions below an obvious rate limit;
6. pressure to reconnect;
7. a recipient not wanting the sender to know they have blocked them;
8. revocation while an event is in transit;
9. suspected account compromise;
10. a lost or stolen phone;
11. a lost or stolen Band;
12. a shared phone or device;
13. gifting without purchaser authority;
14. ownership transfer;
15. inability to access the phone immediately;
16. accidental or deliberate local inhibition; and
17. safe resumption.

For each scenario ask what should happen, who has authority, what the sender
should be told, what could create pressure or harm, whether the operation is
understandable and what could make it unusable.

General customer interviews are not sufficient expertise for
technology-facilitated abuse or coercive-control design. Qualified specialist
review should later address coercive-control patterns, safe block and
notification behaviour, account-recovery abuse, support handling and risks to
people leaving relationships where findings warrant it.

Do not intentionally recruit survivors of abuse merely to test dangerous
scenarios without an appropriately reviewed method.

---

## 11. Accessibility Research

Investigate:

- whether local inhibit can be found without sight;
- operation with reduced dexterity, tremor, limited grip or one hand;
- state communication without relying only on colour, sound, light or haptics;
- deliberate versus accidental activation;
- prevention of accidental resumption;
- cognitive and memory burden;
- whether inhibited state can be determined locally;
- discreet operation;
- charging alignment, force and confirmation; and
- sensory sensitivity to haptic or light output.

Gate 1 identifies requirements and research priorities only. It does not
establish formal accessibility compliance.

---

## 12. Pricing Research

Do not ask only what a participant would pay. Investigate actual recent spending
on wearables, jewellery, meaningful gifts, paired products, accessories,
replacements and subscriptions attached to hardware.

Test purchase context explicitly:

- single Band versus likely need for two Bands;
- self-purchase versus gift;
- purchaser versus recipient;
- included service versus ongoing subscription;
- replacement after loss or damage; and
- perceived durability, quality and support expectations.

After qualitative interviews establish plausible categories, use a structured
price-sensitivity exercise asking when a price appears implausibly cheap, good
value, worthy of serious consideration or too expensive. Rotate or randomise
ranges where appropriate to reduce anchoring. Record pair-versus-single
assumptions.

Gate 1 identifies plausible ranges and contradictions; it does not set a retail
price.

---

## 13. Australian-First Validation

Evidence required to retain or reconsider `DEC-019` includes:

- relevant problems among reachable Australian participants;
- plausible target segments beyond the founder's network;
- metropolitan, regional and remote connectivity and fulfilment considerations;
- preliminary shipping and battery-logistics feasibility;
- support and warranty expectations;
- purchase interest under Australian economic conditions;
- comparison with locally available wearables and meaningful gifts; and
- preliminary mapping of radio, battery, privacy, consumer-law, cybersecurity and product-safety issues.

Possible outcomes are retain, narrow, continue provisionally, defer or supersede
the Australia-first assumption. Gate 1 does not perform or claim final regulatory
assessment.

## 14. Survey Design

Final survey wording must wait until interviews are analysed.

### Behavioural Questions

Cover separation patterns, existing communication, recent unmet moments,
wearable ownership and abandonment, charging behaviour and actual purchase
history.

### Attitudinal Questions

Cover privacy, intrusion, trust, remote-output comfort and maintenance tolerance.

### Concept-Response Questions

Cover sender and recipient interest, distinctiveness, control comprehension,
delayed-event outcomes, delivery feedback and concerns after realistic
constraints are disclosed.

### Price-Sensitivity Questions

Cover single-versus-pair assumptions, gift versus self-purchase, structured price
sensitivity, subscription resistance and replacement expectations.

### Context Variables

Collect only what analysis requires, such as broad age range, broad Australian
location category, relationship context, separation pattern, purchaser or
recipient role, wearable usage and accessibility considerations participants
choose to disclose.

Interview findings must determine answer options, scenario language, relevant
relationship categories, price ranges, objections and protective-control terms.

## 15. Evidence Standards

### Strong Supporting Evidence

- Repeated spontaneous accounts of recent behaviour across independent participants.
- Similar findings across purchaser and recipient roles.
- Behaviour or existing expenditure consistent with stated intent.
- Continued interest after cost, maintenance, privacy, safety and delivery limitations are disclosed.
- Findings reproduced across more than one method.

### Moderate Supporting Evidence

- Consistent stated preferences linked to specific situations.
- Concept responses supported by plausible past behaviour.
- Directional survey evidence consistent with qualitative findings.

### Weak Evidence

- Hypothetical enthusiasm or general emotional agreement.
- Compliments without behaviour or trade-offs.
- Interest before price, consent, maintenance or recipient burden is disclosed.
- Founder-connected participants alone.
- Social-media likes or statements that the idea is cool.

### Contradictory Evidence

- Credible evidence opposing a hypothesis.
- Material sender-recipient asymmetry.
- Methods producing materially different conclusions.
- Strong segment-specific rejection.
- Controls failing to reduce safety concerns.

Contradictory evidence must be retained rather than averaged away.

### Insufficient Evidence

- Samples are too narrow or founder-connected.
- Key recipient groups are absent.
- Interviews were leading.
- Findings cannot be traced to records.
- Survey wording preceded discovery research.
- Results depend on superficial concept enthusiasm.
- Major contradictions remain unexplained.

Participant statements and intended behaviour must be distinguished from observed
or historical behaviour.

## 16. Bias Controls

- **Confirmation bias:** Record hypotheses and falsification conditions before concept testing.
- **Founder bias:** Use a structured guide and obtain an independent review of a sample where feasible.
- **Leading questions:** Ask about recent behaviour before concept exposure.
- **Courtesy bias:** Explicitly request reasons to reject or stop the project.
- **Friends-and-family bias:** Cap and separately identify connected participants.
- **Selection bias:** Track recruitment sources and recruit sceptics, recipients and non-wearable users.
- **Survivorship bias:** Include people who abandoned wearables or similar products.
- **Interpretation bias:** Maintain supporting and contradictory evidence registers.
- **Price anchoring:** Ask purchase history first and rotate structured ranges.
- **Emotional framing:** Use neutral descriptions without romantic imagery or aspirational claims.
- **Interviewer inconsistency:** Use the same core protocol and record deviations.
- **Premature segmentation:** Do not infer a market solely from isolated enthusiasm.

## 18. Ethics and Privacy Safeguards

Gate 1 is currently planned as product and customer discovery. It is not
described or represented as university-approved research.

Minimum safeguards are:

- voluntary, informed participation;
- the ability to skip questions or withdraw;
- separate explicit permission for recording;
- a non-recorded option where practical;
- prompt de-identification of notes;
- no unnecessary traumatic disclosure;
- scenario-based handling of sensitive topics;
- a process for ending an interview if distress occurs;
- incentives that compensate time without becoming coercive;
- no reuse of recruitment contacts for marketing without separate consent;
- defined access, storage, retention, deletion and backup treatment before collection;
- no participant personal data in Git;

requirements must be checked before that activity proceeds. The project must not
claim university approval unless it has actually been obtained.

## 19. Gate 1 Stop and Pivot Criteria

The following require serious review; they are not automatic arithmetic rules:

1. Early interviews produce little spontaneous, recent or behaviourally specific evidence of the proposed problem.
2. Most credible accounts indicate existing communication is sufficient in relevant situations.
3. Recipient interest is materially and consistently weaker than sender interest.
4. Interest falls sharply after realistic consent, maintenance, non-delivery and price constraints are disclosed.
5. Remote physical interaction is repeatedly perceived as intrusive or controlling.
6. Pause, block, permission removal and local inhibit do not materially reduce recipient concern.
7. Participants cannot reliably distinguish protective operations after neutral explanation.
8. The required consent model appears too complex for the value provided.
9. Intended users reject a dedicated wearable or additional charging obligation.
10. Value is not distinct from existing smartwatch or phone haptics.
11. Plausible willingness-to-pay evidence appears incompatible with later preliminary cost reality.
12. Product value depends primarily on sender visibility into recipient delivery or availability.
13. Gifting interest depends on purchaser control or visibility into recipient activation.
14. No relationship context combines meaningful recipient value with plausible commercial reach.
15. Accessibility work identifies no plausible usable local-control direction.
16. Specialist review identifies coercion or safety risks that cannot plausibly be reduced.
17. Australia-first lacks reachable demand or creates disproportionate early barriers.
18. Contradictory evidence remains too substantial to support progression.

As an early diagnostic signal, very limited problem evidence across an initial
qualitative wave should trigger investigation and possible redesign or pause. It
must not be interpreted as a population-prevalence estimate. Final qualitative
conclusions must consider strength, spontaneity, recency, behavioural
specificity, repetition across independent participants, contradictory evidence,
participant diversity and recruitment bias. Interview counts do not establish
statistical significance.

Outcome guidance:

- **STOP:** The core problem or recipient value is weak, or safety cannot plausibly be controlled.
- **PIVOT:** The problem is real but a dedicated Band or remote physical output is not the appropriate solution.
- **PROCEED WITH CHANGES:** Value exists but the segment, scope, interaction, controls or purchase model requires revision.
- **INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE:** Coverage or research quality prevents a decision.

## 20. Gate 1 Success Criteria

Progression to Gate 2 requires all of:

- repeated, spontaneous evidence of a relevant customer problem;
- recent behavioural evidence rather than general enthusiasm;
- independent recipient evidence;
- credible value beyond existing communication and wearables;
- continued interest after realistic constraints are disclosed;
- no unresolved indication that the core experience is broadly intrusive or unsafe;
- comprehension of recipient authority and protective-control intent;
- evidence that phone-independent local control is valued and plausibly usable;
- acceptable wearable and charging tolerance within at least one reachable segment;
- preliminary purchase and price-sensitivity evidence;
- a defensible position on one versus multiple relationships;
- a sufficiently valuable and commercially plausible primary relationship context;
- a reviewed Australia-first assumption;
- documented contradictory evidence and limitations;
- updated risk implications; and
- no unresolved Gate 1 stop criterion.

General enthusiasm cannot satisfy these criteria. Positive results from the five
Early Diagnostic Review interviews cannot justify progression.

## 24. Gate 1 Exit Checklist

Every item must be marked **PASS**, **FAIL**, **INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE** or
**NOT APPLICABLE**.

### Research Integrity

- [ ] Hypotheses and falsification conditions were recorded before concept testing.
- [ ] Problem discovery occurred before concept exposure.
- [ ] The Early Diagnostic Review was not represented as validation.
- [ ] Recruitment sources and biases were documented.
- [ ] Founder-connected participants were identified separately.
- [ ] Recipient roles and wearable sceptics were represented.
- [ ] Contradictory evidence was retained.
- [ ] Research limitations were documented.
- [ ] Personal information was minimised and stored separately.

### Problem Evidence

- [ ] Participants independently described a recurring relevant problem.
- [ ] Evidence included recent behaviour and specific examples.
- [ ] Existing communication was inadequate in identifiable circumstances.
- [ ] Problem importance and recurrence were assessed without treating qualitative counts as prevalence.
- [ ] Evidence was not dependent on concept enthusiasm.

### Concept and Recipient Evidence

- [ ] The concept demonstrated credible value beyond existing phones or wearables.
- [ ] Recipient interest was independently supported.
- [ ] Interest survived realistic constraints.
- [ ] Intrusiveness was assessed.
- [ ] Delayed and non-delivery expectations were assessed.
- [ ] Relationship-cardinality assumptions were assessed.
- [ ] A commercially plausible primary relationship context was identified.
- [ ] Secondary relationship contexts were investigated without making them a survival requirement.

### Consent and Safety

- [ ] Pause, block, disconnect, permission removal, local inhibit and reset comprehension were tested.
- [ ] Former-partner scenarios were assessed through an appropriate method.
- [ ] Repeated interaction and pressure-to-reconnect scenarios were assessed.
- [ ] Protective-status disclosure was assessed.
- [ ] Lost, compromised, replaced, gifted, transferred and shared-device scenarios were assessed.
- [ ] Specialist-review needs were identified.
- [ ] General customer interviews were not treated as specialist abuse expertise.
- [ ] No unresolved safety finding prevents responsible progression.

### Wearable and Accessibility

- [ ] Wearable acceptance was supported for at least one credible segment.
- [ ] Charging and maintenance tolerance were assessed.
- [ ] Wearable sceptics were represented.
- [ ] Early local-control accessibility needs were identified.
- [ ] Accidental activation and resumption were considered.
- [ ] Gate 1 findings were not represented as accessibility compliance.

### Commercial and Market Evidence

- [ ] Actual purchase behaviour was investigated.
- [ ] Price sensitivity was assessed without selecting a retail price.
- [ ] Single-versus-pair assumptions were explicit.
- [ ] Gift and self-purchase contexts were compared.
- [ ] Subscription resistance was assessed.
- [ ] Australia-first evidence was reviewed.
- [ ] Preliminary willingness to pay was not clearly incompatible with continued investigation.

### Decision Quality

- [ ] Every critical hypothesis received a supported outcome.
- [ ] Every high-risk assumption received evidence.
- [ ] Stop and pivot criteria were assessed.
- [ ] Qualitative evidence was assessed by strength and context, not treated as population statistics.
- [ ] Risk-register implications were identified.
- [ ] Requirements and decision implications were identified.
- [ ] The final outcome follows from evidence rather than founder preference.
- [ ] No implementation or technology selection is implied by the outcome.

A Gate 1 outcome cannot be **PROCEED** if a critical item is `FAIL` or
`INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE`. `PROCEED WITH CHANGES` requires explicit ownership and
later-gate treatment for every material change.

## 25. Immediate Research Recommendation

The first research activity, once separately approved, should be five neutral
problem interviews conducted as the **Early Diagnostic Review**.

The diagnostic group should include, where practical:

- at least one participant considering primarily the recipient role;
- at least one wearable sceptic or former wearable user;
- exploration of at least one non-romantic relationship context; and
- participants outside the founder's immediate network.

The review must assess protocol flaws, leading or misunderstood questions,
recruitment problems, unexpected themes and strong negative evidence. It may
justify pausing or redesigning the research. Positive findings cannot validate
the product or justify Gate 2 progression.
